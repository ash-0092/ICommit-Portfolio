### The question Current Value answers

How much value is this product delivering **right now** — to customers *and* to the organisation.

---

### The problem wasn't generating measures. It was selecting them.

The NSM file already held seven: the North Star, three input metrics, three counter-metrics. A scorecard row takes 1–3.

**The selection rule:** the scorecard serves the *investment* conversation — someone deciding whether to keep funding this product. It is not the weekly steering dashboard. A measure that answers *"what do we do next?"* belongs in the NSM file. A measure that answers *"is the value real?"* belongs here.

---

### Argument 1 — Fulfilment rate vs on-time fulfilment

I proposed **fulfilment rate (fulfilled ÷ created)**. Reasoning: it shows users find value and stay committed.

Pushback, two reasons:

1. **On-time maps to the vision, fulfilment rate doesn't.** The vision is *"nobody has to chase."* A commitment fulfilled three weeks late was still kept — but someone chased. Fulfilment rate can't see that failure; on-time can.
2. **Fulfilment rate is confounded by volume.** A casual user who creates 2 and completes 2 scores 100%. A heavy user who creates 20 and completes 15 scores 75%. The better user looks worse.

**Result: swapped to `% of shared commitments fulfilled on or before due date`.**

Fulfilment rate keeps its place as a *guardrail* in the NSM file, catching trivial-commitment inflation. That's a steering job, not an investment-conversation job.

---

### Argument 2 — Should a business measure appear pre-launch?

I argued user behaviour is the honest scope, because without user value there is no business value.

Half right. **EBM defines Current Value as value delivered to customers *and* to the organisation.** A scorecard with no business row can't answer the question it exists to answer.

The fix isn't to invent a revenue number. It's to **declare the gap** — the same move as "To be planned" on the GO roadmap. The row exists, names the measure and the target, and says plainly that it isn't measurable yet.

**Omitting it looks like I didn't think of it. Declaring it looks like a scope decision.**

---

### Side lesson — not every target needs a baseline first

Earlier rule: lock the threshold, then take a baseline, then set a target. That applies to **growth numbers**.

It does *not* apply to **floors**. `>=80% two-party agreed` is a "must not fall below" line — it's a standard you're setting, not an improvement you're forecasting. You can set a floor on day one; you can't set a growth target without knowing where you're starting.

That's why Promise Keepers has `Target: TBC after baseline` while the other two carry numbers.
